Understanding UK Consumer Unit Regulations

Consumer unit rules in the UK sit within BS 7671, the IET Wiring Regulations — currently the 18th Edition incorporating Amendment 4, published April 2026 (the previous Amendment 3 version remains valid during a transition period until 15 October 2026). Three requirements matter most for anyone replacing a fuse board today.

Metal enclosures Reg 421.1.201

Consumer units installed in domestic premises must be made from non-combustible material — in practice, metal — or fully enclosed in a non-combustible casing. The reasoning is straightforward: if a fault inside the unit causes arcing or overheating, a metal enclosure doesn't itself catch fire and contains the problem inside the box, rather than letting it spread to a combustible wall or surrounding material. It's a fire-containment measure, not a style choice, and it's why a wooden or plastic-backed fuse box is one of the clearest signs a board is overdue an upgrade.

Surge protection (SPD) Reg 443.4

Since the 18th Edition, surge protection has been required wherever a transient overvoltage — from a lightning strike or grid switching event — could realistically cause serious harm, loss of essential services, or significant damage to equipment. For most homes, this makes fitting an SPD the standard position. As of Amendment 4 (April 2026), the process for deciding this has also been tightened: the older informal risk-assessment route for skipping surge protection has been removed, meaning any decision to omit it now needs to be a documented, deliberate one rather than a default assumption.

In practice, that means most new or upgraded consumer units in UK homes now include an SPD as standard, protecting the electronics in the property — TVs, computers, smart devices — from voltage spikes on the incoming supply.

Individual circuit protection (RCBOs)

Older boards often protect several circuits through a single shared RCD, which means a fault on one circuit — a faulty appliance, a damaged cable — can cut power to everything on that side of the board at once. Modern consumer units generally use an RCBO per circuit instead, combining overcurrent and earth-fault protection individually, so a fault in one room trips only that circuit and leaves the rest of the house running normally.

Landlords with HMOs, student lets or care accommodation: Amendment 4 also introduced a requirement for arc fault detection devices (AFDDs) on socket circuits in these higher-risk residential categories. If that applies to your property, it's worth raising when you book any consumer unit work.

For the full detail behind these requirements, the IET's own technical FAQ on consumer units is a good primary source — the IET is the body that publishes BS 7671.
