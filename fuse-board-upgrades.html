Old Fuse Box vs Modern Consumer Unit

If your board looks anything like the "old" column below, it's worth having it looked at.

Old Fuse Box

  • • Rewireable fuse wire, or none at all
  • • Wooden or plastic casing
  • • Little or no RCD protection
  • • One fault can cut power to the whole board
  • • No surge protection

Modern Consumer Unit

  • • Individual RCBOs per circuit
  • • Non-combustible metal enclosure
  • • A fault trips only the affected circuit
  • • Built-in surge protection device (SPD)
  • • Meets current BS 7671 requirements

Why This Actually Matters

Since Amendment 3 of the UK wiring regulations, consumer units fitted in homes must be made from non-combustible material — typically metal — rather than the plastic or wooden casings common in older properties. It's a fire-safety rule, not a cosmetic upgrade.

Individual circuit protection (RCBOs) means a fault in one room — a faulty appliance, a damaged cable — only cuts power to that circuit, not your whole house. And surge protection is increasingly treated as standard in modern domestic installations, protecting sensitive electronics from voltage spikes on the incoming supply.

If you've had an EICR come back Unsatisfactory because of your board, or you're just not sure how old yours is, this is usually the single biggest item on a fail — and the one most worth fixing properly rather than patching around.

Common Questions

How do I know if my fuse board needs upgrading?

If your board still uses rewireable fuse wire instead of circuit breakers, has a wooden or plastic (rather than metal) casing, or offers no RCD protection at all, it's overdue an upgrade. An EICR will also flag this directly if you're unsure.

Why does the new consumer unit need to be metal?

Since Amendment 3 of the wiring regulations, consumer units installed in domestic properties must be made of a non-combustible material (typically metal) or fully enclosed in one. It's a fire-safety requirement, not a style choice.

What's the difference between an RCD and an RCBO?

An RCD (residual current device) protects a group of circuits together, so a fault on one can cut power to all of them. An RCBO protects a single circuit individually, so a fault in one room doesn't take out the rest of the house. Modern boards use one RCBO per circuit.

How long does a consumer unit upgrade take?

A standard replacement usually takes half a day to a full day, and typically requires a short power shut-off while the old unit is disconnected and the new one wired in and tested.
